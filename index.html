Bene Gesserit // Diagnostic Tool

Four Fits.

A coaching diagnostic for early-stage propositions. Talk it through in plain words, or fill the four hypotheses directly — a coach walks every box and every connection between boxes, showing you where the joints align and where the model needs revisiting.

Start here // In plain words

Just tell me about it.

No structure needed. Describe your idea the way you'd explain it to a friend — who it's for, the problem, how it might make money. I'll ask a few questions, then draft the four hypothesis cards for you to refine.

Hypothesis // Market

Who, exactly — and how badly does it hurt?

Get to the individual. Find both altitudes of the problem. And be honest about whether it's a painkiller or a vitamin.

Title, company size, geography. The exact human who pulls out the credit card or signs the PO.
The systemic "why it matters" — the big problem the micro problem sits inside.
The specific, addressable bottleneck inside the macro problem. You solve the macro by picking the right micro.
A problem can be specific and still be a vitamin. We want painkillers. Pick honestly, then give the evidence.

02
Hypothesis // Product

What value lands — and can they realize it?

Marketing is a promise. The product has to let them realize it. A concrete, realizable benefit — not a nice-to-have — backed by features that are actually shipped.

The concrete, realizable benefit. Not "beautiful" — something checkable, like "save 2.5 hours a day".
The actual promise you make in marketing — then pick whether it's a value promise or an emotional one.
Value promisenuts-and-bolts — what a startup needs
Emotional promisebrand / feeling — mature-business marketing
The specific features — shipped today, not roadmap — that deliver the value prop and solve the micro problem. They must map back.
What pulls them in? Can it form a new habit, or replace an existing one?
The single, observable moment a user feels the value. If you can't name it precisely, that's the first thing to fix.
How fast from sign-up to Aha. Minutes, hours, days, weeks, months.
Why do they come back tomorrow? Network effect, lock-in, habit, switching cost.
Onboarding's only job is to drive users to the Aha. What's the path? What gets stripped to make it shorter?

Hypothesis // Model

The model is the bet.

Steve Blank: a startup is a temporary entity set up to find a scalable, repeatable business model. Don't dial this in. Price to capture the value you add — and make sure ARPU covers CAC.

Mouse$100 ARPA · ~1M · needs virality
Rabbit$1k ARPA · ~100k · self-serve
Deer$10k ARPA · ~10k · inside sales
Elephant$100k ARPA · ~1k · field sales
Whale$1M ARPA · ~100 · enterprise
Subscription, freemium, transactional, marketplace, razor-and-blade, pay-per-use, revenue share, who-pays inversion…
Should agree with your animal and your channel.
Sometimes the model is the innovation, not the product (Apple's telco-subsidised handset; Xerox per-page billing). If standard, say so — that's valid too.
Best method: ramp price up until they won't pay — find the ceiling. Price to capture the value you add, not to match competitors.

The value you add, in money where possible — $ saved or $ made per customer per year. Your price should be a fraction of this.
Show the arithmetic. The channel must be repeatable and pay for itself.
Diagnostic // Step 05

Walk every box, and every joint.

The coach reads each box on its merits, then walks the connections between boxes — does a shipped feature actually solve the stated problem, does the channel reach the Who at density, does pricing capture the quantified value — and tells you, plainly, whether this fulfils the requirements of Four Fits.

Built on Brian Balfour's Four Fits, Steve Blank, Sean Ellis's Hacking Growth, Christoph Janz's animals, and Strategyzer's model patterns. Tradecraft from years of running these sessions at Dogpatch Labs.
